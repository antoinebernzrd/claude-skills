# Finance lens (always on)

Read this for every take, whatever the subject. Antoine's working assumption is that money explains more than most commentary admits, and he wants that question asked first even when the subject is a war, a novel or a piece of legislation. Applying this lens to a non-commercial subject is often where the take comes from, because most commentary in those domains does not do it.

## The four questions

**Who pays.** Trace the actual cash to its origin. Taxpayers, subscribers, advertisers, LPs, a sovereign fund, a foundation, a subsidy, a debt issue. Not the nominal customer, the ultimate source. When the visible customer is not the payer, that gap is usually the whole story.

**Who profits, and on what timescale.** Separate who benefits from the stated outcome from who benefits from the activity regardless of outcome. Consultants, contractors, intermediaries and incumbents frequently profit from the attempt whether or not it succeeds. That structure predicts behaviour better than stated intent.

**What the capital structure implies.** Equity holders want variance, debt holders want survival, grant funders want reportable outputs, public shareholders want quarters, sovereign backers want strategic position. The financing type constrains what the actor can do, and constrains it more reliably than what they say they want. A venture-funded actor cannot behave like an infrastructure operator even when the asset is infrastructure. That mismatch is a recurring source of tells.

**Who is paying to make you believe this.** Communications budgets, lobbying spend, commissioned research, think tank funding, sponsored studies. Applies to a press release and an op-ed alike.

## Standard tells

- Capex or commitment larger than the stated business could ever service.
- Revenue model absent from an announcement that is otherwise detailed.
- A figure sized for a legislator, a regulator or a county commissioner rather than an investor. Economic impact studies, job creation projections, cost-of-inaction estimates.
- Timing keyed to a budget cycle, an earnings date, a funding deadline or a vote rather than readiness.
- An announcement that functions as a financing instrument: a commitment device to pull subsidy, unlock a tranche, anchor a valuation or pressure a counterparty into moving.
- Cost structure that only works with a subsidy that has not passed yet.
- A protected market being described as a large one. Protection and size are different things and are routinely conflated.

## Sanity checks to run

- Test any big headline number against a real denominator: annual GDP of the region, the total sector budget, the incumbent's revenue.
- Ask what multiple or magnitude is implied, and name the comparable it is being valued against.
- Ask whether the margin structure required has ever been achieved by anyone in that activity.
- Ask what happens to the whole thesis if the subsidy, the policy or the political consensus disappears.
